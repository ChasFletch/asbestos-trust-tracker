# Methodology Source Notes — August 12, 2026

## Federal baseline: GAO-11-819

GAO-11-819, published September 23, 2011, reported that since 1988 **60 trusts** had been established with approximately **$37 billion in total assets**. GAO’s analysis of available payment data found roughly **3.3 million claims valued at $17.5 billion** paid from 1988 through 2010.[1]

These are historical system-level reference points. GAO reviewed 47 financial reports for 2009 and 2010, not an ongoing current-balance census. It therefore cannot substantiate a current “$30 billion available” claim.[1]

For audit practice, GAO reported that officials at **2 of the 11** trusts it interviewed described random and targeted review samples. One of those trusts described an external audit that sent a sample of X-rays to an independent doctor. GAO also reported that none of the officials who discussed audits indicated that the audits had identified fraud.[2]

## Historical third-party benchmark: ILR’s 2018 report

The U.S. Chamber Institute for Legal Reform published *Dubious Distribution* on March 8, 2018. The report describes its own historical compilation through year-end 2016: about **$40 billion** contributed from 2004 through 2016, **nearly $25 billion** in assets at year-end 2016, and more than **$2 billion** in deferred funding obligations.[3]

Because the report was commissioned by an advocacy organization and covers a defined historical interval, it should be shown as a dated, third-party benchmark—not as a current balance or as a replacement for filed trust figures.

## Bates White / Mealey’s provenance

A March 2013 House Judiciary witness statement by Bates White economist Marc Scarcella identifies the original source trail: it cites the 2012 Mealey’s article *Asbestos Bankruptcy Trusts: A 2012 Overview of Trust Assets, Compensation & Governance*. The statement says that, after more than $14 billion in 2006–2011 claim payments, confirmed trusts retained **more than $18 billion** in assets and had an additional **$11–12 billion in proposed trust assets pending confirmation**. That combination is the documented source of the approximate $30 billion historical shorthand.[4]

It is not a current-balance figure: it combined confirmed assets with projected funding of unconfirmed reorganizations, and it was explicitly a 2012–2013 snapshot. The 2013 statement identifies the pending reorganizations as W.R. Grace, Pittsburgh Corning, NARCO, Flintkote, Congoleum, Quigley, Plant Insulation, A.P. Green, and Durabla.[4]

The verifiable propagation record is more nuanced than a single origin. A 2011 House hearing includes witness Lester Brickman’s attribution that RAND had determined trusts had “north of $30 billion available.” The 2013 Bates White testimony supplies a different, identifiable construction: more than $18 billion in confirmed assets plus $11–12 billion in pending proposed funding. In 2019, Brickman cited the 2013 Mealey’s overview while restating that remaining trust assets totaled more than $30 billion after more than $15 billion had been paid.[5] Modern law-firm SEO pages often restate “$30 billion available” without preserving any date, source, or distinction between confirmed assets and pending funding. This record supports describing the phrase as a chain of dated, non-comparable historical claims—not as a current audited total.

A 2015 Bates White / Mealey’s commentary separately reports approximately **$23.4 billion** of funding received, **$17.0 billion** in claim payments, and **$18.6 billion** in confirmed trust assets at year-end 2013, plus **$160 million** deferred funding. It also states that several large 2014 confirmations were expected to provide more than $8 billion in additional funding.[6]

The available original materials support a progression of historical funding, assets, and payouts, but no source reviewed in this pass states a current “$30 billion available” balance. The public methodology should therefore describe the phrase as an unsupported marketing shorthand rather than attribute it to a simple GAO subtraction.

## U.S. Trustee Program hearing record

In the official June 8, 2017 House Judiciary hearing record, U.S. Trustee Program Director Clifford J. White III stated: “As a watchdog of the system, when you look at post-confirmation trusts, and asbestos trusts in particular, it is very clear there is no independent policeman. There is no watchdog.” The statement concerns post-confirmation oversight and should be attributed to White in the hearing record, not presented as a general empirical finding about a particular trust.[7]
